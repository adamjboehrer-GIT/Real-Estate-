# Seller-Side Transaction Playbook

<div class="subtitle">From accepted offer to recorded deed. Coastal Orange County and North San Diego County.</div>

<div class="callout">
<p class="ct">The one idea this document is built on</p>
<p>On the listing side your job after acceptance is not to celebrate the price. It is to <strong>defend the price you got, protect the seller's net, and keep the seller out of a lawsuit after close.</strong> Those are three different jobs and only one of them ends at recording.</p>
<p>The listing agent carries a liability the buyer's agent does not. Buyers sue sellers over undisclosed conditions for years after closing, and when they do, the listing agent is named. Everything in the disclosure section of this document is written with that in mind.</p>
</div>

## How to use this

Work top to bottom on a live listing. The phase sections are chronological. The question banks are meant to be read out loud, verbatim, on the phone. The deal-killer table is worth re-reading at day 10, when you still have time to act on it.

Two standing rules:

**Rule 1: Calendar from the executed contract, never from this document.** Every day count below is a printed default on the C.A.R. Residential Purchase Agreement, and every one of them is a negotiated fill-in blank. C.A.R. has materially revised the RPA three times in the last eighteen months. Build the calendar from the signed contract in front of you.

**Rule 2: Nothing in California happens automatically.** A passed deadline does not remove a contingency, does not cancel the contract, and does not release the deposit to your seller. A buyer who blows past day 17 without signing a Contingency Removal is still fully protected, and can cancel on day 30 and take their deposit with them. Freeing your seller requires a Notice to Buyer to Perform, then a Cancellation. In writing, delivered, both times.

## Phase 1 — Days 0 to 3: Take control immediately

Speed here is a strategic weapon, not just good manners. Everything you do in the first 72 hours either builds or destroys the buyer's agent's confidence in you, and you will need a favor from that person around day 25.

### Deliver disclosures on day 1 or 2, not day 7

This is the highest-leverage single habit on the listing side and most agents get it wrong. Delivering early:

- Starts the buyer's review clock immediately, so their contingency work compresses into the front of the window instead of the back
- Kills the day-25 renegotiation attempt, because "we just found this out" stops being available to them
- Removes the buyer's statutory right to cancel for late delivery
- Signals to the other agent that you run a tight file, which changes how they treat you for the rest of the deal

### The disclosure conversation with your seller

This is the conversation that keeps your seller out of court and your name off a complaint. Have it early, and have it seriously.

<div class="script">
<p class="ct">The disclosure conversation. Say this.</p>
<p>"I want to be direct about how this part works, because it is the one part of this transaction that can follow you after you have the money. California law requires you to disclose everything you know that could affect the value or desirability of this house. Not just what is broken now, what you <strong>know</strong>. That includes things you fixed, things you fixed yourself without a permit, leaks that stopped, neighbor disputes, an incident in the house, work that was done before you owned it that you learned about."</p>
<p>"Here is the rule I want you to use: <strong>if you are wondering whether to disclose it, that is the answer. Disclose it.</strong> A disclosed problem almost never kills a deal. An undisclosed one that a buyer finds two years later becomes a lawsuit, and it costs multiples of what the repair ever would have. There is no version of this where hiding something works out."</p>
</div>

### The rest of the first 72 hours

1. **Confirm the acceptance date in writing** with the buyer's agent, along with the resulting contingency dates.
2. **Deliver the contract to escrow and title** and confirm a human has it.
3. **Make the wire fraud call to your seller.** They are receiving a large wire at close and they are a target. Same script, same discipline as the buyer side.
4. **Verify the deposit actually hit escrow.** Get written confirmation from the escrow officer. Do not take the buyer's agent's word for it.
5. **Call the buyer's lender yourself.** This is the single largest risk in the deal and a listing agent who does not do this is flying blind. Script is in the question bank.
6. **Order the HOA documents and certification immediately.** HOA management companies are the most reliably slow party in any Southern California escrow and will delay your close by a week if you let them.
7. **Order the NHD report** if it is not already done.
8. **Calendar everything** with 3-day and 1-day alerts.
9. **Update the seller's net sheet** with the actual accepted terms, including any credits agreed. Sellers anchor to the number you gave them at listing, and every credit after that feels like a loss unless you re-baseline it in writing.

### Pre-frame the repair request now

Say this on day 3, before it happens. A seller who has been warned negotiates calmly. A seller who is blindsided gets angry and does something that costs them money.

<div class="script">
<p class="ct">Pre-framing the Request for Repair</p>
<p>"Around day twelve to fifteen you are going to receive a repair request. I want to tell you now what it is going to feel like, so it does not catch you off guard. It is going to be a long list, it is going to feel personal, and it is going to look far worse than it actually is. Every house on this coast generates one of these, including brand new construction. My job when it arrives is to separate what is real from what is noise, and I will have a plan and a number for you when it comes. Do not read it and respond. Read it and call me."</p>
</div>

## Phase 2 — Days 3 to 17: Manage, do not wait

### Be present at the buyer's inspection

Or have a specific reason not to be. You want to know what they found on the same day they found it, which gives you 48 hours to get your own contractor's bid before the request arrives. Walking into a repair negotiation with a competing bid already in hand changes the entire conversation.

### Monitor the buyer's performance actively

You are tracking four things and you are tracking them yourself:

1. Deposit in escrow, confirmed in writing
2. Loan file in underwriting, with conditions shrinking rather than growing
3. Appraisal ordered, inspected, and reviewed
4. Contingency removal on schedule

If any of the four goes quiet, you call. A buyer's agent who cannot answer where the loan stands is telling you something important.

### The appraisal is your job

Not the lender's, not the buyer agent's. Meet the appraiser at the property, and bring a packet:

- Your comps with the reasoning written out, especially any comp that supports the price and is not obvious
- A list of upgrades with dates and actual costs
- The floor plan and square footage source
- Any survey or lot information
- Offer history showing multiple bids, if there were any

In pocket neighborhoods with thin comps, which describes most of San Clemente and effectively all of Laguna, the appraiser genuinely needs the help and it is entirely appropriate to give it. This is not influence, it is providing data.

Note the current RPA allows a buyer to write the appraisal contingency as an **appraisal gap** rather than all-or-nothing. Read that paragraph carefully on every offer you receive, because it determines who eats a low appraisal and how much of it, and that changes how you evaluate competing offers.

### Respond to the repair request with a counter

Never a flat yes, never a flat no, and never slowly.

Your leverage is real and worth understanding: by the time that request arrives the buyer has spent one to two thousand dollars on inspections, has told their family they bought a house, and is emotionally in the home. Your seller's leverage is highest right at this moment and it declines every day afterward.

Reasonable posture, in order of preference:

1. **A credit instead of repairs** wherever possible. It caps the seller's exposure at a known number, avoids the seller warranting workmanship, and gets the deal past this point cleanly.
2. **Repair only what the lender will require** to fund, plus genuine health and safety.
3. **Decline cosmetic and deferred maintenance**, politely, in writing, once.

### If the buyer misses a date

Serving a Notice to Buyer to Perform is housekeeping, not hostility, and a competent buyer's agent expects it. But:

- **Never serve one without your seller's explicit instruction.** It is the seller's contract, not yours.
- **Never serve one when you actually want the deal to close** and the buyer is 24 hours behind on something harmless. You will burn goodwill you need later.
- The NBP does not cancel anything. It starts a short clock, typically 2 days, after which the seller has the *right* to cancel by signing a separate Cancellation of Contract. Two documents, two steps.

## Phase 3 — Days 17 to 27: Protect the close

### Payoffs

- Confirm escrow has ordered **every** payoff demand, not just the first. HELOCs get forgotten constantly.
- Confirm the demand actually came back and matches expectations.
- **Solar:** if it is financed, the loan gets paid off. If it is PACE or HERO, it is a tax-lien-position assessment that most lenders require be cleared at close, and it comes straight out of the seller's net. If it is leased or PPA, the buyer must be approved to assume it and that takes weeks. Know which one on day 1.

### HOA

Documents, certification, and transfer fees. Ordered early, followed up weekly. This is the most common cause of a one-week delay in a coastal condo transaction and it is entirely preventable.

### Repairs, done and documented

Complete all agreed repairs **before the walkthrough**, with receipts and, where applicable, licensed contractor invoices. A failed walkthrough two days before close is a self-inflicted wound and it hands the buyer leverage at the exact moment your seller has none.

### Move-out and rent-back

If the seller needs to stay past close, that is a documented rent-back: form SIP for 30 days or less, or a separate lease agreement over 30 days. Never a handshake. Confirm the insurance question is answered, because the seller's homeowner's policy ends at close and the buyer's policy does not cover the seller's belongings.

### Keep updating the net sheet

Every credit, every repair, every extension changes the seller's number. Send an updated net sheet after every change. Sellers who get one surprise at the signing table remember it forever.

---

## Phase 4 — Close and after

- Confirm **funding** and then confirm **recording**. Two different events. Only recording transfers the property, and the seller's proceeds wire typically goes out on recording.
- Keys, remotes, gate fobs, mailbox keys, pool keys, HOA cards and codes, all delivered.
- Seller's utilities cancelled effective the recording date, not before.
- Confirm the proceeds wire went out and that your seller received it. Then confirm again by phone, because this is the second wire fraud exposure point in the transaction.
- Remind the seller about the tax reporting items below, and send them to their CPA.

# Question banks

## Ask the buyer's lender — this is the call most listing agents never make

Introduce yourself on day 2 or 3. Be friendly, be direct, and call weekly after that.

<div class="qa">
<ul>
<li>Is this a fully underwritten pre-approval, or a pre-qualification? Which one?</li>
<li>What loan product and what is the down payment percentage?</li>
<li>Have you verified income and assets, or is that still to come?</li>
<li>Is the file in underwriting today, or still in processing?</li>
<li>What conditions are outstanding, and who owes them?</li>
<li>Is the appraisal ordered? Has the appraiser physically been out? Has the lender reviewed it?</li>
<li>Are there any gift funds, retirement withdrawals, or asset sales involved that need seasoning?</li>
<li>Is the buyer's purchase contingent on anything you are aware of, like the sale of another property?</li>
<li>What is your honest confidence that this closes on the contract date?</li>
<li>Is there anything about this file you would want to know if you were on my side of it?</li>
</ul>
</div>

## Ask the buyer's agent

<div class="qa">
<ul>
<li>Confirming acceptance date and contingency dates. Do you agree?</li>
<li>Is the deposit wired? Can escrow confirm it today?</li>
<li>What inspections are they ordering, and when are they scheduled?</li>
<li>Has your buyer seen the disclosures? Any questions so far?</li>
<li>Is your buyer selling or leasing something that has to happen first?</li>
<li>Have they secured an insurance quote yet?</li>
<li>What is your buyer's biggest hesitation right now?</li>
<li>Is there anything on your side that would keep us from closing on time?</li>
</ul>
</div>

The insurance question matters more than it used to. The current RPA is explicit that the loan contingency does not cover insurance problems, so an uninsurable property is a problem that surfaces late and hard.

## Ask the escrow officer

<div class="qa">
<ul>
<li>Is the deposit in? Please send me written confirmation.</li>
<li>What is outstanding from my seller, and what is outstanding from the buyer?</li>
<li>Have all payoff demands been ordered? How many liens are showing?</li>
<li>Is there a HELOC, a solar loan, or a PACE assessment on this file?</li>
<li>Have HOA documents and the transfer certification been ordered, and when?</li>
<li>Is title clear, and if not, what is required to clear it and who is doing it?</li>
<li>Do you have compensation instructions from both brokerages?</li>
<li>What withholding forms does my seller need to complete, and by when?</li>
<li>What day does my seller sign, and will they need a mobile notary?</li>
</ul>
</div>

## Ask the title officer

<div class="qa">
<ul>
<li>Are there any exceptions on this prelim that will be a problem for the buyer's lender?</li>
<li>Are there liens, judgments, or child support liens against my seller?</li>
<li>Is vesting clean, or is there a trust, a deceased spouse, or a former spouse still on title?</li>
<li>Is there a solar UCC-1, a PACE or HERO assessment, or a private transfer fee?</li>
<li>Any recorded violations or open permits?</li>
</ul>
</div>

The vesting question catches the deal-killer that surfaces at day 25: a trust that was never properly funded, an ex-spouse who never signed an interspousal transfer deed, or a deceased co-owner whose interest was never cleared. All three take weeks to fix and all three are findable on day 2.

## Ask your seller, at listing and again at acceptance

<div class="qa">
<ul>
<li>Walk me through everything that has ever been repaired, replaced, or altered on this property, including anything you did yourself.</li>
<li>Was any of that work done without a permit?</li>
<li>Has there ever been a leak, a flood, mold, a pest issue, or a foundation concern, even one that was fixed?</li>
<li>Any disputes with a neighbor, the HOA, or the city? Any boundary or easement issue?</li>
<li>Has anything happened in this house that a buyer would want to know about?</li>
<li>Is the title held in a trust or an entity, and is anyone on title unavailable, deceased, or a former spouse?</li>
<li>Are you a California resident for tax purposes? Are you a US citizen or resident alien? (This drives withholding.)</li>
<li>Where are you going, and when can you actually be out?</li>
<li>What is the number you need to walk away with, and does it still work with what we just accepted?</li>
</ul>
</div>

## Ask HOA management

<div class="qa">
<ul>
<li>Are there special assessments in effect, approved, or under discussion?</li>
<li>Is there pending or threatened litigation?</li>
<li>Has the SB 326 balcony and elevated element inspection been completed, and what were the findings?</li>
<li>Are there open violations against this unit?</li>
<li>What are the transfer fees and the document turnaround time?</li>
<li>Is my seller current on dues and assessments?</li>
</ul>
</div>

# What kills deals, and what you control

| Deal killer | Early warning sign | What you control | The mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buyer's loan collapses | Lender is vague, "processing" past day 7, conditions multiplying | Whether you are watching at all | Call the buyer's lender on day 2 and weekly after. Insist on a fully underwritten pre-approval at offer stage, not a pre-qual. |
| Low appraisal | Thin comps, record price, unique property | Preparation | Meet the appraiser with a comp packet. Understand the appraisal gap language in the offer before you accept it. |
| Repair request escalates | Older home, deferred maintenance, seller who deferred | Framing and speed | Pre-frame it on day 3. Get your own bid before responding. Counter with a credit, fast. |
| Undisclosed condition surfaces | Seller who says "they do not need to know that" | The conversation | Have the disclosure conversation seriously and early. If in doubt, disclose. This is a post-close liability, not just a deal risk. |
| Late disclosure delivery | Waiting until day 7 | Entirely yours | Deliver on day 1 or 2. It removes the buyer's late-delivery cancellation right and kills the day-25 renegotiation. |
| HOA documents delay close | Nobody ordered them early | Entirely yours | Order day 1. Follow up weekly. Never assume management is moving. |
| Title or vesting defect | Trust, probate, divorce, deceased co-owner | Early reading | Read the prelim day one. Ask the vesting question directly. These take weeks to cure. |
| Payoff surprise | HELOC, solar loan, PACE | Asking | Ask escrow to confirm every lien. Identify solar type on day 1. |
| Seller cannot move out | Vague answers about the next home | Asking directly and early | Ask at listing. Paper any rent-back with SIP or a lease. Never a handshake. |
| Failed walkthrough | Repairs left to the last week | Scheduling | Complete repairs a week early, with receipts. |
| Seller net shock | Credits and repairs accumulate quietly | Communication | Updated net sheet after every single change. |
| Buyer blows contingency date and lingers | Day 18 with no CR | Process | Talk to your seller. Extend or serve an NBP. Nothing happens on its own. |
| Wire fraud on proceeds | None | The conversation | Two calls: one at open, one before funding. The seller is a target too. |

# Details that get lost

**Disclosure and liability**

- Every repair, alteration, and unpermitted improvement, written down
- Prior leaks, mold, pest, or foundation work, even if resolved
- Neighbor, HOA, boundary, or easement disputes
- Deaths on the property within the statutory lookback period, and how to handle the question correctly
- Anything the seller learned about the property from a prior owner
- Your own AVID, completed honestly from your own visual inspection, not copied from the seller's

**Money and tax**

- **FIRPTA withholding** if the seller is a foreign person. This is a federal withholding obligation and it is the buyer's liability if missed, which means it becomes everyone's problem.
- **California Form 593 withholding** for out-of-state sellers, with exemptions that must be claimed properly.
- **Section 121 capital gains exclusion** eligibility. Two of the last five years as a primary residence. Sellers often do not know where they stand and it is a very large number.
- **Prop 19 base-year transfer** for 55+ sellers buying a replacement home. Genuinely valuable and almost never raised by the listing agent. Raise it, then send them to their CPA.
- Updated net sheet after every credit, repair, and extension
- Proration of taxes, HOA dues, and any rent
- HOA transfer fees, document fees, and who pays them per the contract
- Compensation instructions delivered to escrow from your brokerage

**Property and logistics**

- Personal property in or out: appliances, window coverings, TV mounts, shelving, patio furniture
- Water heater strapping, smoke and CO detectors, plus any city point-of-sale requirement
- Agreed repairs completed with receipts before the walkthrough
- Every key, remote, fob, mailbox key, pool key, HOA card and code
- Alarm and smart-home account transfers
- Utilities cancelled effective the recording date, not the day the seller moves out
- Mail forwarding

# Special situations

**Trust sales.** Confirm the trust was actually funded, meaning the property was properly deeded into it. Confirm who the current acting trustee is and that they have authority. Get the certification of trust to escrow early. An unfunded trust is a probate, and a probate is months.

**Probate.** Full versus limited authority under the Independent Administration of Estates Act changes everything about the process, including whether court confirmation and overbidding are required. Establish which one before you accept an offer, not after.

**Divorce.** Both parties on title must sign. Confirm the marital settlement agreement addresses the sale and the proceeds split, and get the interspousal transfer deed situation clarified with title on day 1.

**Tenant-occupied.** Notice requirements for showings and for termination are strict in California and vary by local ordinance. Confirm the tenancy status, the lease term, any local just-cause rules, and how the security deposit transfers. Rent proration is a real line item and it gets forgotten.

# Southern California specifics

**Mello-Roos and 1915 Act bonds.** Talega, Rancho Mission Viejo, Ladera Ranch, parts of Ocean Ridge. Separate statutory disclosure. It can add several hundred dollars a month to the buyer's payment, which affects their debt-to-income and can blow up their approval late in escrow. Know the total assessment on your listing before you take an offer.

**SB 326 balcony and elevated element inspections.** Every condo HOA in California has had to inspect elevated wood elements, and coastal salt air means findings are common and repairs are expensive. If your listing's association has a pending balcony special assessment, that is disclosable and it is far better delivered on day 1 than discovered by the buyer on day 20.

**Coastal Zone permitting.** Much of San Clemente, Dana Point, Laguna, Carlsbad, and Oceanside is in the Coastal Zone. Unpermitted additions, decks, and seawall work are live issues and a Coastal Development Permit is not a formality. If your seller says "we added the deck ourselves," that is a permit search and a disclosure, not a footnote.

**Bluff and slope.** San Clemente has a documented landslide and bluff-retreat history, and the rail corridor stabilization work has kept it in the news. On bluff-adjacent and hillside listings, expect the buyer to order a geotechnical review and prepare your seller for what it may say.

**Insurance and fire zones.** Buyers in canyon and open-space-adjacent areas are increasingly getting declined by admitted carriers and pushed to the FAIR Plan plus a DIC wrapper. This is now a legitimate reason a qualified buyer fails to close on your listing. Ask the buyer's agent about insurance in week one so you find out early rather than at day 25.

**Solar.** Owned, financed, leased, and PPA are four different transactions with four different closing paths. Determine which one your listing has before it goes live, not after you have an offer.

# C.A.R. form cheat sheet
