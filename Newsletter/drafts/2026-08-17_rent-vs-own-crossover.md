## Where this came from

The original idea was: show what someone who bought last year at 6-and-change has in
equity today versus someone still renting. So I ran it.

It did not say what that framing assumes. Over 14 months, **renting won.** Not close.

I could have written the equity-looks-great version, and every number in it would have
been technically true, because $446,000 of equity is a real figure. It would also have
been misleading, because 6% costs of sale erase the gain on a hold that short and the
monthly cost of owning here is roughly double the cost of renting.

So the piece became the honest version, which is better anyway: renting wins early,
owning wins later, here is where the line is and here is what actually moves it. Nobody
else in this market is publishing that. Most of them are still publishing "buy now or be
priced out forever," and readers can smell it.

## The math

### Part 1: the 14-month scoreboard

| | Bought June 2025 | Waited, buying now |
|---|---|---|
| Price | $1,830,000 | $1,892,500 (+$62,500) |
| 20% down | $366,000 | $378,500 (+$12,500) |
| Rate | 6.82% | 6.67% |
| Principal + interest | $9,564/mo | $9,739/mo (+$176) |
| All in (P+I, tax, ins, maint) | $13,000/mo | $13,285/mo |

The 2025 buyer today: home worth $1,892,500, loan down to $1,445,936, so **$446,564 of
equity** against $366,000 in. That is $62,500 of appreciation plus $18,064 of principal.
They have also paid $115,828 in interest.

Sell today and 6% costs of sale return $333,014 on $366,000 in. The paper gain is real.
The realized gain, at 14 months, is not there yet.

Renting the same house at roughly $6,300/mo cost about $86,100 over the same period with
zero equity, but the owner spent about **$6,850/mo more** than the renter, which is
$95,893 of extra outlay. And the renter's $366,000 stayed invested.

**At 14 months, renting won.** The lead says so out loud. That is the credibility move.

### Part 2: the crossover

Buying today at $1,892,500 and 6.67%, versus renting the same house at $6,300 with the
down payment invested at 7%:

| Appreciation assumption | Rent growth | Buying pulls ahead in |
|---|---|---|
| 0%, worst case | 5.77% | never within 30 years |
| 3.56% (SC 20-yr actual, includes the 2008 crash) | 4.38% (latest YoY) | year 20 |
| 3.56% (SC 20-yr actual) | 5.77% (SC 11-yr actual) | **year 16** |
| 5.00% (middle case) | 5.77% | year 9 |
| 7.90% (SC 10-yr actual) | 5.77% | year 3 |

### Why it flips

The mortgage payment does not move. $9,739/mo in year 1 is $9,739/mo in year 20.
Property tax is capped at +2%/yr on the assessment. Rent is capped at nothing.

San Clemente rent, actual: **$1,846/mo in July 2015 to $3,420/mo in July 2026.**
5.77% a year, every year. Run that forward on a $6,300 house: $8,340 in year 5,
$11,040 in year 10, $19,346 in year 20.

That is the whole mechanism. Not appreciation, not timing the rate. A payment that
freezes versus a payment that compounds.

### What the answer is sensitive to

Worth knowing which inputs are load-bearing, because the range is wide and pretending
otherwise is how these pieces lose people.

| Input | Moves breakeven from year 16 to |
|---|---|
| Rent $5,500 instead of $6,300 | year 20 |
| Rent $7,500 instead of $6,300 | year 12 |
| Renter earns 4% on cash, not 7% | year 11 |
| Renter earns 9% on cash | never within 40 years |
| Maintenance 0.5% of value, not 1% | year 13 |

Two honest notes on the model, both of which make owning look worse, not better:
it charges the owner a full 1%/yr maintenance accrual while the renter's rent covers
maintenance invisibly, and it models no mortgage interest deduction at all.

> ### I ran the numbers on renting versus buying here. Renting won.
>
> Then I ran them with a longer horizon and it flipped.
>
> Here is the setup. Someone bought at the San Clemente single-family median in June of
> last year. $1,830,000, twenty percent down, 6.82 percent. Someone else looked at the
> same house, decided to wait for rates to come down, and kept renting.
>
> Fourteen months later.
>
> The buyer's home is worth $1,892,500. The loan is down to about $1,446,000. On paper
> that is roughly $446,000 of equity against the $366,000 they put in.
>
> Sounds like a win. It isn't one yet.
>
> That house costs about $13,000 a month to own once you count the loan, the taxes, the
> insurance, and the money you quietly set aside for whatever breaks next. Renting the
> same house runs somewhere around $6,300. Over fourteen months the owner spent about
> $96,000 more than the renter did. Sell today, and six percent in costs of sale eats the
> appreciation whole.
>
> At fourteen months, renting won. I am not going to pretend otherwise.
>
> Meanwhile the rate everyone was waiting on went from 6.82 to 6.67. Fifteen hundredths
> of a point, over fourteen months, and the house got $62,500 more expensive while they
> waited. The payment on it today is higher, not lower.
>
> Now here is the part that doesn't make it into most newsletters.
>
> The mortgage payment never moves. $9,739 a month in year one is $9,739 a month in year
> twenty. Property taxes are capped at two percent a year. Rent is capped at nothing, and
> San Clemente rent has gone from $1,846 to $3,420 in eleven years. Just under six percent
> a year, every year, without a single headline about it.
>
> Run that forward. The $6,300 house rents for $11,000 in ten years. Nineteen thousand in
> twenty.
>
> That is what flips it. Not appreciation. Not timing the Fed. One payment that freezes
> and one that compounds.
>
> So where is the crossover? Use San Clemente's twenty-year appreciation rate, the one
> that includes the 2008 crash, assume the renter invests the down payment and earns seven
> percent on it, and buying pulls ahead in year sixteen. Use the last ten years of actual
> local appreciation instead and it is year three.
>
> That is a wide range and I would rather hand you the range than a fake number. It depends
> on what you believe about the next decade.
>
> The practical version is simpler, and it has nothing to do with rates.
>
> If you are going to be here three years, rent. The math is not close, and anyone telling
> you otherwise is selling something.
>
> If you are going to be here fifteen, buy when the right house shows up and stop watching
> the Fed.
>
> The question was never whether it is a good time to buy. It is how long you are staying.

## Verify Before Publishing

1. **The rent number, $6,300/mo. This is the one that matters.** It is currently an
   estimate, not a source. Zillow's rent index for San Clemente reads $3,420, but that
   index blends condos and apartments, so it is not the rent on a house comparable to the
   $1.89M median. Public rental portals all block automated reads. The defensible fix is a
   CRMLS closed-lease pull for San Clemente single-family, which needs Adam logged in.
   Sensitivity: $5,500 moves the crossover to year 20, $7,500 moves it to year 12. The
   lead's structure survives either way, but the printed figures need to be right.
2. **Refresh the market data.** Newest local data in the repo is the July 28 deck and June
   2026 closings. An August deck and a fresh InfoSparks San Clemente SFR pull should land
   before send so the issue is not quoting three-week-old numbers.
3. **Confirm the 20-year and 10-year appreciation figures** are labeled as Zillow Home
   Value Index for San Clemente single-family mid-tier, not CRMLS. They are two different
   sources and the footnote has to say which is which.
4. **Freddie Mac rate as of send date.** 6.67% is the week of August 13, 2026. Recheck the
   morning of the send.
5. **Sources footnote** must carry: CRMLS via InfoSparks for medians, Freddie Mac PMMS for
   rates, Zillow ZORI for rent, Zillow ZHVI for appreciation, and the standing line that
   loan and tax details are general information rather than lending or tax advice. The
   piece states a Prop 13 assessment cap, which is factual, but the footnote should keep
   the no-tax-advice line.
