For First-Time Buyers

A home that earns its keep: a realistic way onto the South Orange County coast.

If you have quietly written off buying here, give me ten minutes. There are two versions of ownership most people never consider. One where the house helps pay for itself, and one where the house is cheap because nobody else wants to do the work. Here is how both actually play out in South OC, what the numbers look like, and where to start.

The Idea

You might not be priced out. You might be looking at the wrong kind of home.

Living on this coast almost feels unfair.

Almost.

Beach days run long. The weather barely changes. Something new opens up between Dana Point and Oceanside every month.

Then you open Zillow at 11pm and there it is. A two-million-dollar shack built in 1964. And the question arrives on its own. Will we ever actually buy a house here?

Yes.

Prices on this coast are real, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. But most people who assume ownership here is off the table are picturing exactly one thing: a finished single-family house they cover entirely on their own. That is the single hardest version of this. Of course it looks impossible. You are looking at the most expensive way to do it.

There are two other versions. The first is a home that earns its keep, the setup you might have heard called house hacking. You buy a property with more than one living space, live in one part, and rent out the rest. That rent goes straight at your mortgage every month, which can turn a payment that felt impossible into one that pencils. It is how a lot of people quietly got their first foothold in expensive markets, and it works especially well on a coast where rents are strong.

The second is buying the house nobody else wants to fix. That one is worth its own section, and it is further down this page, because right now it might be the bigger opening of the two.

If staying on this coast is the priority, owning here is not out of reach. It may just take a shift in what you picture. Not the white-picket-fence, single-family house you always imagined, but a home where someone else rents a part of the property. That one trade is what turns priced out into possible.

What It Looks Like

Three shapes it takes here.

  • A duplex. The cleanest version. Two units under one roof, you live in one and rent the other. A handful trade in San Clemente in any given stretch, and more show up in Oceanside, which is the most attainable entry point on this part of the coast.
  • A single-family home with a legal ADU or casita. Same idea, different wrapper. You live in the main house and rent the accessory unit, or the reverse. California has spent the last few years making these dramatically easier to build and rent.
  • A junior ADU (JADU). A small second unit carved out of the existing house, often a bedroom with its own entrance and kitchenette. The lowest-cost way to create rentable space if a home does not already have it.

Why It's Reachable

You don't need to be an investor with piles of cash.

This is the part most people do not realize. Because you live in one of the units, the property still counts as your primary home, so you can use an owner-occupied loan instead of an investor loan. That changes everything about the down payment and the approval.

Most people assume you need 20 percent down to buy anything on this coast. You do not. Because you live in one of the units, the property counts as your primary home, which opens up owner-occupied loan programs built for exactly this and gets you in for far less than 20 percent down. On top of that, a lender can usually count a good share of the expected rent from the other unit toward what you qualify for. So the rental side helps you twice: it helps you get approved, then it helps you pay. A lender will lay out the specific programs and what each one costs, and that is a conversation worth having early.

Here is the part that should change how you see it. A lot of people on this coast are already paying a mortgage-sized rent check every month, real money that builds a landlord's equity instead of their own. A home that earns its keep aims that same money at something you own, with a tenant helping to carry the payment.

How much the rent offsets depends entirely on the property. Nothing here is a guarantee, and a lender plus real local comps will give you the true picture before you commit.

One tip if you are weighing sizes: a duplex is usually the simplest place to start. Two-unit homes tend to come with the friendliest financing and the least red tape, while triplexes and fourplexes bring extra lender hurdles that are harder to clear in a high-cost area like ours. For a first move, two units is the sweet spot.

Tailwind

California just made the ADU path easier, again.

If you go the accessory-unit route, the rules moved in your favor. A batch of new state ADU laws took effect January 1, 2026, and a few matter directly for this coast:

  • Coastal permitting got faster. A 2025 law streamlines the coastal development permit process for ADUs inside the coastal zone, which covers a lot of South OC. That has historically been one of the slowest parts of building here.
  • Junior ADUs got more flexible. The owner-occupancy requirement was eased for JADUs that have their own bathroom, and the rules were cleaned up to match standard ADUs.
  • Smaller units got cheaper to build. ADUs under 500 square feet are now exempt from school impact fees, which trims the upfront cost of adding a rentable unit.

None of this makes an ADU free or instant. It does mean the door is more open than it was even a year ago.

The Other Opening

Nobody wants a project right now. That is the opening.

This is the one I am watching in real time, and for a first-time buyer it might be the best thing happening on this coast.

Buyers right now want turn-key. Dialed in, nothing on the list, move the couch in on Saturday. Anything that needs work sits.

That is not a mild preference. Zillow puts the gap between homes that need work and move-in-ready homes at roughly 14 percent, and that discount has nearly doubled in the past year. A survey of more than two thousand homeowners found 62 percent bought move-in ready against 28 percent who took on a fixer. Among first-time buyers over the last five years, close to two-thirds went move-in ready.

Now read those numbers from the other side.

The pool of people willing to run a renovation has thinned out. The homes that need work are still sitting there, and they are getting cheaper relative to everything finished around them. Fewer bidders on the same house is not a warning sign. It is leverage, and right now it belongs to whoever is willing to pick up a paintbrush.

Slow is fine, too. You do not have to gut a house in six months. Buy something structurally sound in a location you actually want, live in it, and fix one thing at a time as cash allows. Kitchen this year. Bathrooms in two. Every dollar of that work lands on a home you own, in a town where the finished version of that same house sells for meaningfully more.

Everyone else is bidding on the finished house. The discount is sitting on the one that needs a weekend.

Stack it with the rest of this page and it gets more interesting. A duplex that needs cosmetic work. A home with a tired casita. Now you are buying the condition discount and the rental income at the same time, and both of them are working on the same mortgage.

Cosmetic and structural are completely different animals. Dated kitchens, old flooring, bad paint, popcorn ceilings: that is the good kind of problem, mostly time and money you control. Foundation movement, roof, failing plumbing or electrical, additions that were never permitted: that is the kind that eats the discount and keeps eating. The inspection matters more on these homes than on anything else you will look at, and I want a contractor's number in your hand before you write the offer, not after.

The 14 percent figure is national, from Zillow. I cannot cleanly reproduce it for San Clemente, because the MLS carries no condition field to sort on, so treat it as the shape of the trend rather than a local measurement. What I can tell you is what I see in the field: the turn-key homes here are moving, and the project homes are the ones taking the price cuts.

The Payoff

What you actually get out of it.

  • Someone else covers part of the payment. Every rent check is a chunk of your mortgage you are not carrying alone.
  • You build equity instead of paying a landlord's. The years you would have spent renting are years you are on the ownership side of this coast, which has been one of the more durable places to own in California.
  • There are real tax advantages. The rented portion opens up deductions and depreciation that a plain primary residence does not. Your accountant is the right person to map this to your situation.
  • It sets up your next move. A few years in, when you are ready for something bigger, you can move on and keep the place as a rental that pays you every month. That first home, the one that earned its keep, becomes the foundation.

Where to Start

Five steps to find out if this is your path.

  1. Get a real pre-approval from the right lender. Not every loan officer has done owner-occupied two-to-four unit deals. Find one who has, and ask specifically how they will count the rental income. This one conversation tells you your actual range.
  2. Pick your town honestly. Oceanside is the most attainable entry on this coast and has the most small multi-unit inventory. San Clemente and San Juan Capistrano run higher but do have duplexes and ADU-ready homes. We match the town to your number, not the other way around.
  3. Look at duplexes and homes with rentable space. That means true two-unit properties plus single-family homes with a legal ADU, a casita, or the room to add one. It is a smaller pool than the regular market, so it pays to have someone watching it for you.
  4. Widen the search to the homes that need work. The listings sitting longest and cutting price most are often the ones that just need cosmetics. Tell me you are open to a project and I will send you the ones other buyers are scrolling past, plus an honest read on which repairs are cheap and which ones are not.
  5. Run honest numbers before you fall in love. Real market rent for the second unit, real payment, real reserves for the months a unit sits empty. If it works on paper the way you need it to, then you move.

Straight Talk

A few honest caveats.

Both of these are genuinely good strategies, and neither one is magic. Small multi-unit inventory on this coast is thin, so the right property takes patience. You become a landlord, which means the occasional 9pm text about a water heater. Rents, loan rules, and city ADU requirements change, so every number here needs to be checked against a lender and the specific city before you count on it. And a unit will sit empty now and then, so you build a cushion for that.

On the fixer side, be honest with yourself about appetite, not just budget. Renovation costs more and takes longer than the plan, every time. Living through the work is harder than it sounds. And a discount only becomes equity if the work actually gets done, so the strategy rewards people who will genuinely chip away at it rather than people who like the idea of it.

None of that changes the core point. For a lot of people who think this coast is out of reach, buying a little differently is the difference between renting for another decade and owning now.
